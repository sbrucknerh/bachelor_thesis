\chapter*{Abstract}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Malicious activity on Ethereum can involve transaction sequences that are
difficult to recognise from individual address summaries. This thesis examines
whether temporal motif fingerprints improve malicious-address detection, how
their usefulness depends on the temporal window, and whether they distinguish
reported behaviour categories. The fingerprints count each address's local
roles in ordered three-transfer candidate patterns. They are evaluated on two
labelled Ethereum datasets using held-out addresses, nine windows from five
minutes to 30 days, and seven orderings of transfers with equal timestamps.

Adding fingerprints to 17 conventional transaction and graph features increases
average precision in all six cohort comparisons on the main dataset, but only
three increases are statistically significant. The independent binary comparison
also shows no significant improvement. Larger windows generally increase both
average precision and fingerprint coverage, without establishing a unique
optimum. Across nine malicious-behaviour categories, fingerprints contain
statistically detectable category information, but classification remains
limited, with macro average precision of 0.1953. Selecting separate windows for
the categories gives no significant improvement over a shared window.

The results show that temporal motif fingerprints describe behavioural
differences, but do not consistently add predictive value beyond conventional
features. The evaluation concerns retrospective address classification, not
direct detection of independently verified coordinated campaigns.


\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
Blockchain networks have become a critical part of the global financial infrastructure. As they have grown, they have also become an attractive target for criminal actors. According to the Chainalysis 2025 Crypto Crime Report, more than \$40.9 billion flowed to illicit cryptocurrency addresses in 2024 alone \citep{chainalysis2025, shi2025realcats}. This number is likely a lower bound. Chainalysis notes that annual illicit-volume estimates have grown by an average of 25\% between reporting periods since 2020, so the 2024 total could exceed \$51 billion \citep{chainalysis2025}. The abuse ranges from ransomware and phishing scams to large-scale money laundering, and it affects both the Bitcoin and Ethereum ecosystems \citep{shi2025realcats, sanjalawe2023atd}.

The harder problem is not the size of these losses, but the way they are produced. Sophisticated attackers do not rely on a single, continuously active address. Instead, they spread their activity over many short-lived wallets, where each wallet makes only a few transactions that look unremarkable on their own. In fact, 90.70\% of criminal Bitcoin addresses carry a zero balance and 60\% of them stop being active within 31 days \citep{shi2025realcats}. This behaviour deliberately imitates ordinary wallets, so a single transaction rarely looks suspicious. The signal of coordinated activity therefore does not appear in individual transactions, but in how those transactions are connected across several addresses and over time. A ransomware payment chain, a phishing fan-out or a layering operation only becomes visible once its transactions are considered together, in order, and across all the addresses involved.

Detecting these patterns requires a representation of how transactions connect addresses over time. Feature-engineering methods summarise transaction histories through statistics such as transaction count, address lifetime and balance variance \citep{chen2022phishing, alemari2021labeled}. These summaries do not explicitly count ordered interactions involving several addresses. Other approaches do incorporate time, through timestamp-aware network embeddings or models of changing transaction neighbourhoods \citep{wu2020phishers, ghoshcatalog2025}. Temporal motif features have also been applied to Ethereum phishing detection \citep{wang2023temporal}. The question is therefore not whether time or motifs have been used before, but whether a local motif-role fingerprint adds predictive information beyond conventional address features under the evaluation settings considered here.

Temporal motif fingerprints are a natural way to address this gap. They describe how an address participates in short, ordered transaction patterns within a bounded time window \citep{kovanen2011temporal, paranjape2017motifs}. Before comparison with a random-network reference, these patterns are candidate motifs. A motif in the statistical sense is a pattern that occurs significantly more often than expected under such a reference. This thesis counts candidate patterns and evaluates their usefulness for supervised detection. The term \emph{motif fingerprint} names this representation and does not imply that its components have passed a test of overrepresentation. Earlier work on Bitcoin found cyclic triangle patterns to be more frequent than in the communication networks examined \citep{paranjape2017motifs}. Whether per-address fingerprints improve the supervised detection of malicious behaviour remains the question addressed here.

This thesis investigates the following research questions (RQs):

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1.] Do temporal motif fingerprints improve the detection of
          malicious Ethereum addresses beyond transaction-based aggregates
          and static graph features?
    \item[RQ2.] How does the temporal window size ($\delta$) affect the usefulness of temporal motif fingerprints for detecting coordinated malicious behaviour?
    \item[RQ3.] Do different categories of coordinated malicious behaviour exhibit distinct temporal motif fingerprints?
    \item[RQ4.] Does selecting a separate temporal window for each behaviour
          category improve category classification compared with using one
          shared window?
\end{description}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:relatedwork} reviews graph-based detection methods, the available transaction datasets, and previous work on temporal motifs. Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} introduces the blockchain and graph-theoretic concepts needed to define temporal patterns, local roles, and address fingerprints. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} describes the data preparation, graph construction, feature extraction, and evaluation protocol. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the findings for the four research questions. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets these findings, relates them to earlier work, and discusses the limitations of the study. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarises the conclusions and outlines directions for further research. Appendix~\ref{app:engineering_challenges} records the engineering challenges and implementation decisions behind the pipeline.

\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:relatedwork}

Research on malicious blockchain activity differs in what is represented and
what is predicted. Some methods classify addresses from transaction summaries,
while others learn from their neighbourhoods or from ordered sequences of
transfers. The datasets also differ in whether labels describe transactions,
addresses, trust relations, or particular types of abuse. This chapter reviews
these approaches and explains how the present evaluation relates to them.

\section{Graph-Based Detection of Malicious Behaviour}
\label{sec:related_graph_detection}

A transaction graph provides more context than a list of independent payments.
However, the choice of graph determines which interactions are visible.
\citet{lee2020ethereum} study several Ethereum interaction networks, covering
ordinary transactions, contract interactions, and token transfers. Their
comparison shows why the graph representation must be specified before its
properties can be interpreted. This is relevant here because an ordinary
transaction graph and a graph that also includes token transfers do not describe
the same set of activities.

Early supervised detection methods use address-level summaries of such
networks. \citet{ostapowicz2019detecting} compare random forests, support vector
machines (SVMs), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) on more than 300,000
Ethereum accounts. They also examine how individual features affect the
classification. Their work establishes a practical starting point for the
present thesis: conventional address features already contain useful detection
information, so any additional representation needs to be tested against them.

\citet{chen2022phishing} construct 149 features from an address's direct
transactions, drawing on patterns associated with fraudulent bank accounts.
Their method filters the features and uses a random forest for phishing
detection, reporting 98.89\% precision on their dataset. The features have a
clear connection to transaction behaviour, but they summarise the target's
activity rather than count ordered interactions among several addresses.

\citet{sanjalawe2023atd} also derive features from a weighted directed
multigraph. Their approach combines feature selection with a semi-supervised
generative adversarial network and reports 95.06\% detection accuracy.
The selected features include structural measures and summaries of transaction
timing. This is different from representing the exact order of transfers in a
small connected subgraph. The reported scores from these studies cannot be
compared directly with each other or with the present results, because the
datasets, class proportions, and evaluation procedures differ.

Together, these studies motivate the conventional feature baseline in this
thesis. Transaction counts, values, activity durations, and static graph
properties can identify differences between malicious and benign addresses.
The purpose of adding local motif-role counts is to test whether short
transaction sequences contribute information beyond these summaries.

\section{Network Embeddings and Time-Aware Learning}
\label{sec:related_temporal_learning}

Network embeddings replace a manually designed feature vector with a learned
representation of an address and its neighbourhood.
\citet{wu2020phishers} introduce trans2vec for Ethereum phishing detection.
The method incorporates transaction amounts and timestamps into biased random
walks and then applies a one-class support vector machine. It therefore already
uses temporal information. Its representation is learned from walks, however,
rather than expressed as separate counts of predefined local roles.

Time-aware graph modelling is also studied outside fraud classification.
\citet{wu2020temporal} represent Ethereum records as a temporal weighted
multidigraph and compare walk strategies that account for transaction times and
amounts. Their evaluation concerns temporal link prediction. This supports the
importance of retaining repeated and timestamped interactions, but link
prediction is a different task from deciding whether an address is malicious.

Graph neural networks provide another way to combine an object's features with
information from its neighbours. In the Bitcoin setting,
\citet{weber2019bitcoin} compare graph convolutional networks with conventional
classifiers on the Elliptic dataset. Random forests perform strongly in their
experiments. The result is a useful reminder that a graph-based model does not
automatically improve on a feature-based classifier, even when the underlying
data is relational.

More recent Ethereum work models changing neighbourhoods explicitly.
\citet{ghoshcatalog2025} combine local and global temporal dependencies for
phishing detection. Their model processes sequences of transaction
neighbourhoods and learns how behaviour changes over time. This differs from a
fixed catalogue of short event patterns, but it rules out a general claim that
earlier blockchain detection methods ignore time.

The distinction relevant to this thesis is therefore between representations,
not simply between methods that use time and methods that do not. Temporal
motif fingerprints provide explicit counts for particular ordered patterns.
Embeddings and neural models learn their representations from the data. The
present experiments compare fingerprints with conventional features. They do
not benchmark all of these learned alternatives or isolate the causal
contribution of temporal ordering.

\section{Datasets for Blockchain Transaction Analysis}
\label{sec:relwork_datasets}

A useful detection dataset needs both behavioural observations and labels for
the objects being classified. It also needs sufficient transaction detail for
the proposed representation. An address-level table of summary features cannot
by itself reconstruct the order of interactions between several addresses.

\citet{alemari2021labeled} introduce the Benchmark Labelled Transactions of
Ethereum (BLTE) dataset as a public resource for evaluating detection methods.
The later study by \citet{sanjalawe2023atd} describes its source as Ethereum
Classic and uses abnormal-address information from EtherScamDB. BLTE is relevant
as an established transaction-based benchmark. Its source coverage and binary
labels must nevertheless be distinguished from those of a mainnet Ethereum
address-classification task.

The Elliptic dataset released by \citet{weber2019bitcoin} contains more than
200,000 Bitcoin transactions connected by directed payment flows. Its nodes
represent transactions, not persistent Ethereum addresses. It supports the
study of illicit transaction detection over time, but its graph construction
and prediction unit differ from those used here.

The Real-world dataset of Cryptocurrency Addresses with Transaction Profiles
(Real-CATS), introduced by \citet{shi2025realcats}, combines 103,203 criminal
addresses and 106,196 benign addresses across Bitcoin and Ethereum. Criminal
labels are drawn from abuse reports, while benign addresses are collected from
exchange customers. Alongside address profiles, the release contains
transaction histories that support alternative feature extraction. Its
Ethereum categories make it useful for the category comparisons in this
thesis. These labels describe reported abuse, however, and do not establish
that every address in a category belongs to one coordinated campaign.
A supplementary dataset of cryptocurrency addresses and transaction profiles,
named Sup-CATS, provides a separate deployment-oriented evaluation sample.

The Ethereum Fraud Dataset by Activity provides another source of transaction
histories and address labels \citep{fesevu2025ethfraud}. The release includes raw
transfers with sender, receiver, timestamp, and transaction metadata, together
with labels assembled from Etherscan and curated sources. This makes it
possible to compute conventional features and temporal fingerprints from the
same underlying observations. Its role in the present study is as a data
resource, not as evidence for the performance of a particular detection method.

These resources are complementary rather than interchangeable. Their labels
refer to different objects, and their transaction histories cover different
parts of the underlying networks. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} therefore separates
the role of each source from the selection and graph-construction rules used
in the experiments. Comparisons between feature sets use the same addresses
and data splits, so that differences in their scores are not caused by changing
the evaluation sample.

\section{Temporal Motifs and Local Role Representations}
\label{sec:related_temporal_motifs}

Motif analysis begins with the idea that small patterns can reveal structure
that is not captured by single-node statistics.
\citet{milo2002network} identify network motifs through overrepresentation
relative to randomised networks. Counting a candidate pattern and establishing
that it is statistically overrepresented are therefore separate steps.
\citet{McDonnell2014} make a further distinction between a global subgraph
count and a node's participation in its local roles. Their motif-role
fingerprints describe the positions occupied by individual nodes in directed
patterns. This provides the basis for an address-level representation rather
than a single summary of the entire transaction graph.

\citet{kovanen2011temporal} extend pattern analysis to event sequences whose
similarity depends on both topology and temporal order.
\citet{paranjape2017motifs} develop an efficient counting framework for
$\delta$-temporal motifs and show that pattern counts and relevant time scales
differ across networks. In this framework, an ordered sequence must fit within
a bounded time span. The present thesis uses this counting definition while
referring to untested patterns as candidate motifs. Their predictive value is
evaluated without claiming network overrepresentation.

Several studies connect these ideas to financial behaviour.
\citet{lal2021money} examine temporal cycles in Ethereum money trails and find
different cyclic patterns associated with suspicious activities. Their
heuristic analysis concerns paths through which funds move. It supports
examining connected transfer sequences, but it is not the same evaluation as
testing a fixed local-role fingerprint against a conventional feature baseline.

\citet{wu2022mixing} combine temporal and attributed heterogeneous patterns
with other transaction features to detect Bitcoin mixing services. They use
positive and unlabelled learning because reliable negative labels are limited.
This is direct evidence that motif-based features have already been used in
supervised blockchain analysis. The target is membership in a mixing service,
which is different from the binary and category labels considered here.

There is also a direct Ethereum precedent.
\citet{wang2023temporal} study phishing detection using temporal motif
features extracted from transaction subgraphs. The combination of blockchain
data, labelled phishing addresses, and temporal motif features is therefore
not new. The present work builds on this line of research through its
evaluation of local-role fingerprints, temporal-window sensitivity, and
differences between recorded behaviour categories.

\citet{arnold2024motifs} analyse three-transfer patterns on two Bitcoin
datasets and one non-fungible token (NFT) transaction dataset. Their results
show that global counts can be dominated by a small number of participants or
short periods of activity. Local signatures and pattern completion times
reveal information that these global totals hide. They also examine randomised
timestamp references and the ambiguity of ordering transactions within a block.
These observations motivate both the address-level fingerprints and the
equal-timestamp sensitivity checks in this thesis. Their analysis is primarily
descriptive, rather than a held-out comparison of malicious-address classifiers.

Outside blockchain networks, \citet{liu2023temporal} evaluate temporal motif
features for fraud detection on Mercari transactions and a synthetic
J.P.\ Morgan Chase network. They report improvements over simple graph
features and the embedding methods Large-scale Information Network Embedding
(LINE) and node2vec. Their Venmo analysis addresses different prediction and
description tasks. These findings support the usefulness of temporal patterns
in financial networks, but do not establish that the same improvement will
hold for Ethereum addresses.

A different use of motifs appears in \citet{mo2022motifgcn}.
The authors combine motif matrices and temporal graph convolutional learning
on the Bitcoin-Alpha and Bitcoin over-the-counter (Bitcoin-OTC) signed trust
networks. Their revised model predicts the signs of trust links. The motifs
describe structure within snapshots, while temporal information is combined
across snapshots. These are neither Ethereum payment histories nor direct
counts of ordered three-transfer occurrences, so the task and representation
need to be kept separate from the present approach.

\section{Position of This Thesis}
\label{sec:related_position}

The reviewed literature already contains time-aware embeddings, temporal graph
models, and motif-based blockchain detection. The contribution of this thesis
is an evaluation of a particular local motif-role representation under a common
protocol. It compares conventional features, fingerprints alone, and their
combination on the same held-out addresses. It also examines how the permitted
time span affects the representation, whether recorded abuse categories are
separable through fingerprints, and whether category-specific windows improve
on a shared window.

These comparisons address the four research questions introduced in
Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}. The evaluation also records how the results
change when transfers with equal timestamps are ordered differently. Its scope
is the predictive usefulness and stability of the fingerprints under the
available labels and graph coverage. It does not establish that temporal order
alone causes an improvement or that the counted candidates are statistically
overrepresented network motifs.

\chapter{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{ch:foundations}
This chapter introduces the theoretical foundations of the thesis.

\section{Blockchain and Ethereum Fundamentals}
The first section explains what a blockchain is and how Ethereum handles transactions.
A blockchain is a distributed ledger in which transactions are grouped into blocks and linked chronologically so that later blocks reference earlier ones through cryptographic hashes \citep{nakamoto2008bitcoin, buterin2014ethereum}. This makes the ledger append-only, because changing a past record would require rewriting the block and all of the blocks that follow under the network’s consensus rules \citep{nakamoto2008bitcoin}. For this thesis, the important part is not the consensus mechanism itself, but that blockchain data is ordered, time-stamped, and persistent.

Ethereum extends this basic blockchain idea with account-based state and smart-contract execution \citep{buterin2014ethereum}. The relevant transaction record can be represented as a tuple $(s,r,v,t,g)$, where $s$ is the sender, $r$ the receiver, $v$ the transferred value, $t$ the timestamp, and $g$ the gas usage \citep{alemari2021labeled, sanjalawe2023atd}. Ethereum distinguishes between externally owned accounts (EOAs), which are controlled by private keys and initiate transactions directly, and contract accounts, which execute code when invoked \citep{buterin2014ethereum, alemari2021labeled}.

Because multiple transactions can occur between the same pair of addresses at different times, with different values and gas costs, Ethereum is naturally modelled as a weighted temporal directed multigraph \citep{kovanen2011temporal, paranjape2017motifs}. This is the abstraction used in the remainder of the chapter. In contrast, Bitcoin follows an unspent transaction output (UTXO) model, where transactions consume and create unspent outputs rather than directly transferring value between persistent accounts \citep{nakamoto2008bitcoin}.

With this data model established, the next section introduces the formal graph representation.

\section{Graph-Theory Foundations}
\label{sec:graph}

A graph is a mathematical structure consisting of a set of nodes $V$ and a set 
of edges $E$, where nodes represent entities and edges represent pairwise 
relations between them \citep{newman2010networks}. In the context of this
thesis, nodes correspond to Ethereum addresses and edges correspond to 
transactions between them.

Because transactions have a clearly defined sender and receiver, the 
appropriate structure is a \emph{directed graph}, where an edge $(u, v) \in E$ 
represents a directed relation from node $u$ to node $v$ 
\citep{newman2010networks}. Since the same pair of addresses may transact
multiple times, each with a distinct value and timestamp, the data is more 
precisely represented as a \emph{directed multigraph}, in which multiple edges 
between the same ordered pair of nodes are permitted 
\citep{paranjape2017motifs, sanjalawe2023atd}.

For a node $v$, the \emph{in-degree} $d^-(v)$ counts the number of incoming 
edges and the \emph{out-degree} $d^+(v)$ counts the number of outgoing edges 
\citep{newman2010networks}. These degree measures serve as basic node-level
features and are used as baselines in the classification experiments in 
Chapter~\ref{ch:method}.

The directed multigraph provides the structural foundation for the graph model 
used throughout this thesis. The following section extends this static 
representation by incorporating the timestamps of transactions, which is 
necessary to capture the temporal ordering of interactions.


\section{Temporal Networks}
\label{sec:temporal_networks}
The graph model introduced in Section~\ref{sec:graph} treats the Ethereum transaction graph as a static object once timestamps are discarded. This section explains why that is insufficient for detecting coordinated malicious behaviour.

\paragraph{From static to temporal.}
Static networks aggregate all interactions into a single, time-independent structure. While such aggregation is computationally efficient, it completely ignores the ordering of events, which is exactly the information that distinguishes coordinated sequential behaviour from coincidental events. Temporal networks take this information into account by retaining the full sequence of timestamped edges \citep{kovanen2011temporal, paranjape2017motifs}.

\paragraph{Time-respecting paths and edge ordering.}
A directed time-respecting path follows edges in both their transfer direction
and their temporal order \citep{kovanen2011temporal}. With distinct timestamps,
a path from $v_0$ to $v_k$ is a sequence of the form
\[
    (v_0,v_1,t_1),\ldots,(v_{k-1},v_k,t_k),
    \qquad t_1 < \cdots < t_k.
\]
For example, a transfer
$A \to B$ at 10:00 followed by $B \to C$ at 10:05 forms a time-respecting path
from $A$ to $C$. Reversing the times leaves the same static edges, but removes
this path. The first ordering permits a relay interpretation, although it does
not prove that the same funds were forwarded.

Transfers sharing a block timestamp need an additional ordering rule, since the
timestamp alone does not determine which came first. The treatment of these ties
is described in Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}. Candidate temporal patterns
are not restricted to paths. Their edges must form a connected graph when edge
directions are ignored, but a star with only incoming transfers is also a valid
candidate structure \citep{paranjape2017motifs}.

\paragraph{The time window $\delta$.}
Temporal order alone does not limit how far apart the transfers may occur.
This thesis follows the total-span condition of \citet{paranjape2017motifs}.
For a candidate sequence of $l$ edges, the first and last timestamps must satisfy
\[
    t_l - t_1 \leq \delta.
\]
The bound applies to the whole sequence, not separately to each gap between
successive edges.

A sliding window enforces this bound as the ordered transfers are processed.
For a new transfer at time $t$, earlier transfers before $t-\delta$ can no
longer form a valid candidate ending at that transfer. The interval
$[t-\delta,t]$ therefore moves with the event sequence. These windows overlap
rather than dividing the history into separate fixed bins. A candidate may
cross an hour or day boundary as long as its total span remains within
$\delta$ \citep{paranjape2017motifs}.

Small windows restrict candidates to short bursts, while larger windows admit
interactions spread across days or weeks. No single interval is assumed to
suit every behaviour. This thesis compares nine windows from five minutes to
30 days, with the grid given in Section~\ref{sec:temporal_window_sweep}.
The window is selected on development data without using the held-out test
results, as described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}. Its value limits
the span of a three-transfer candidate, not the duration of a malicious campaign.

\paragraph{What static projection loses.}
The aggregated static graph loses two things: the \emph{ordering} of edges and the \emph{temporal density} of interactions. This means that two structurally identical subgraphs in the static graph could correspond to entirely different behaviours depending on whether the underlying transactions occurred rapidly or were spread across a longer time span. \citet{kovanen2011temporal} observe that static networks are often time-aggregates of systems where connections are not continuously active, and that temporal inhomogeneities, which static representations do not capture, have an important effect on dynamic processes. In the Ethereum context, a fan-out pattern in the static graph (one address sending to many others) looks the same regardless of whether all transfers happened within ten seconds or over ten months. The temporal graph preserves the timing needed to assess whether the fan-out could reflect coordinated activity.

\paragraph{Motivation for temporal motifs.}
The observation that edge ordering matters and that meaningful patterns must occur within a bounded time window motivates the concept of temporal motifs introduced in Section~\ref{sec:motifs}. Rather than asking which subgraph topologies are over-represented in the static graph, temporal motifs ask which \emph{ordered sequences} of interactions recur within a window $\delta$. This is the appropriate question for detecting coordinated behaviour in blockchain networks, where the timing of transactions is as informative as their structure.

\section{Temporal Motifs}
\label{sec:motifs}

\paragraph{Static network motifs.}
The concept of network motifs was introduced by \citet{milo2002network} to describe small, recurring subgraph patterns that appear significantly more often in a real network than in a randomised reference. These patterns are considered the building blocks of network structure, so that different classes of networks (biological, social, technological) tend to be characterised by different sets of over-represented motifs \citep{milo2002network}. In static graphs, a motif is formally defined as a connected induced subgraph on $k$ nodes whose frequency in the observed network exceeds what would be expected by chance under a suitable null model \citep{milo2002network, newman2010networks}.


\paragraph{$\delta$-temporal motifs.}
\citet{paranjape2017motifs} extend the static motif concept to temporal networks by introducing $\delta$-temporal motifs.
A $k$-node, $l$-edge $\delta$-temporal motif is an ordered sequence of
$l$ timestamped edges involving $k$ nodes. The graph formed by these edges must
be connected, and the time between the first and last edge must be at most
$\delta$. Two occurrences represent the same motif when their nodes can be
relabelled without changing the direction or temporal order of the edges
\citep{paranjape2017motifs}.

\paragraph{Candidate patterns and statistical motifs.}
The temporal counting framework defines patterns by their structure, edge order,
and time span. Counting an occurrence does not itself establish that the pattern
is more common than expected by chance. In this thesis, an untested pattern is
therefore a \emph{candidate motif}, or a temporal subgraph. The terms
\emph{motif role} and \emph{motif fingerprint} refer to the features derived from
these candidates. Statistical overrepresentation would require a separate
comparison with randomised networks under a specified null model
\citep{milo2002network}. The supervised tests used here assess prediction and
category separability, not network overrepresentation.

\paragraph{The catalogue of $\delta$-temporal motifs.}

For motifs with three edges on two or three nodes,
\citet{paranjape2017motifs} enumerate a complete catalogue of 36 patterns.
It contains four \emph{2-node motifs}, in which all three edges occur between
the same pair of nodes, 24 \emph{star motifs}, in which one centre node
interacts with two distinct neighbours, and eight \emph{triangle motifs}, in
which every pair of the three nodes is connected. Only two of the eight
triangle motifs form a directed cycle. The individual patterns also differ in
the direction and temporal ordering of their edges. The full catalogue is
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:motifs_paranjape}.

\subsection{Counting Algorithm}

Motif counting algorithms form the computational foundation of any motif-based analysis. This section first outlines the established methods for static graphs and then explains why a fundamentally different approach is required for temporal data.

\subsubsection{Static Motif Counting}

Static graph motif counting is a well-studied problem. Given a graph $G$, the goal is to count how many times a given subgraph pattern, such as a triangle or a directed cycle, appears in $G$. Algorithms for this task range from exact neighbourhood-intersection methods to matrix-based approaches and sampling-based approximations, covering a broad spectrum of accuracy and efficiency trade-offs \citep{Yin2025}. These methods are computationally mature and underpin many downstream tasks such as clustering coefficient computation and community detection.

However, static methods operate on a time-aggregate representation: all edges that ever existed between two nodes collapse into a single connection, regardless of when they occurred \citep{kovanen2011temporal}. This means that the temporal ordering is lost entirely. \citet{paranjape2017motifs} show that even when a triangle is topologically present in a network, only a subset of its instances are temporally valid. The others either occur in a different order or fall outside a given time window $\delta$. A static counter treats all of them as identical. For blockchain transaction networks, this distinction is critical because coordinated malicious behaviour happens through transaction sequences that have a specific temporal order, something that static aggregation cannot recover.

\subsubsection{Temporal Motif Counting}
Temporal motif counting extends the static counting problem by requiring that all edges of a motif occur in a specific order within a bounded time window $\delta$. 

The general counting algorithm proceeds in three steps: (1) identify all
static instances of the subgraph $H$ in the static graph $G$,
(2) for each instance, gather all corresponding temporal edges into a
time-sorted sequence $S$, and (3) process $S$ within a sliding window of
size $\delta$, tracking how often each ordered edge pattern appears
within that window \citep{paranjape2017motifs}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:paranjape}
formalises this procedure.

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\delta$-Temporal Motif Counting adapted from \citet{paranjape2017motifs}}
\label{alg:paranjape}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Time-sorted sequence $S = (e_1, \ldots, e_L)$,
         window $\delta$, motif length $l$
\Ensure Count of each $l$-edge $\delta$-temporal motif in $S$
\State $\textit{left} \gets 1$
\State $\textit{patternCounts} \gets \emptyset$
\For{$\textit{right} = 1, \ldots, L$}
    \While{$t_{\textit{right}} - t_{\textit{left}} > \delta$}
        \State \Call{RemoveFromWindow}{$e_{\textit{left}}$}
        \State $\textit{left} \gets \textit{left} + 1$
    \EndWhile
    \State \Call{AddToWindow}{$e_{\textit{right}}$}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\textit{patternCounts}$
\end{algorithmic}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}
\noindent\textbf{Procedure} \textsc{RemoveFromWindow}$(e)$
\hfill\textit{// expire $e$ and withdraw prefix contributions}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\textit{patternCounts}[e] \gets \textit{patternCounts}[e] - 1$
\ForAll{suffix $\sigma$ of length $< l-1$ in $\textit{patternCounts}$}
    \State $\textit{patternCounts}[(e,\,\sigma)]
           \mathrel{-}= \textit{patternCounts}[\sigma]$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}
\noindent\textbf{Procedure} \textsc{AddToWindow}$(e)$
\hfill\textit{// admit $e$ and extend all existing prefixes}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\ForAll{prefix $\pi$ of length $< l$ in $\textit{patternCounts}$,
        longest first}
    \State $\textit{patternCounts}[(\pi,\,e)]
           \mathrel{+}= \textit{patternCounts}[\pi]$
\EndFor
\State $\textit{patternCounts}[e] \gets \textit{patternCounts}[e] + 1$
\end{algorithmic}

\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}
\end{figure}
 
For 2-node motifs this runs in 
$O(m)$ time, which is optimal. For 3-node, 3-edge star and triangle motifs, specialised algorithms reduce the worst-case complexity significantly, with the fast triangle algorithm achieving up to a 56.5$\times$ speedup over the general method on the Bitcoin transaction network \citep{paranjape2017motifs}. This scalability is relevant to the present work, as blockchain transaction graphs are large and the motifs we are interested in correspond directly to 3-node star and triangle patterns within a short time window.

\subsection{Motif Fingerprint}
The identification of recurring subgraph patterns in a network provides insight
into the local structural properties of individual nodes. A global count records
how often a pattern occurs anywhere in the graph. It does not say which position
an individual node occupies. The \emph{motif-role fingerprint} of
\citet{McDonnell2014} retains this distinction by collecting counts for the local
roles of a focal node, such as an initiator, intermediary, or receiver.

\citet{McDonnell2014} further show that functional motif-role fingerprints, obtained from counts of partial subgraphs, can be transformed into structural fingerprints by multiplying them with an invertible transformation matrix, establishing a mathematically well-defined correspondence between the two representations. The authors identify anomaly detection and the identification of structurally similar nodes as primary use cases, which makes it applicable to this thesis.

The concept is extended here to temporal graphs following the
$\delta$-temporal counting framework. A global candidate pattern first defines
the ordered transfers. Choosing a focal address then determines its local role
in that occurrence. Counts of these roles form the address fingerprint.
Table~\ref{tab:motif_terminology} distinguishes these levels.

For the full local catalogue used in this thesis, the raw fingerprint is
defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_v^{\delta}
      = \bigl(c_{v,1}^{\delta},\,c_{v,2}^{\delta},\,\ldots,\,c_{v,40}^{\delta}\bigr)
    \label{eq:motif_fingerprint}
\end{equation}

where $c_{v,j}^{\delta}$ counts the number of candidate occurrences in which
address $v$ participates in local role $j$ within a span of at most $\delta$.
The 40 components are local motif-role counts, not 40 global patterns or 40
motif classes. Raphtory reports 24 star-centre roles, eight two-node roles, and
eight triangle roles. Section~\ref{sec:motifcountingandretainedroles} explains
their relationship to the 36 global patterns and the 32-role subsets used when
triangle coverage is incomplete. A feature matrix has one row per address and
one column per retained local role.

This serves two purposes. First, it reduces the high-dimensional transaction graph to a compact, interpretable, and fixed-size representation per address. Second, it encodes behavioural structure rather than raw transaction statistics because an address involved in coordinated activity is expected to exhibit a distinct fingerprint compared with ordinary addresses, as coordinated behaviour by definition produces recurring, time-constrained interaction patterns among a group of addresses. The fingerprint therefore creates a behavioural signature of each address showing small recurring temporal subgraph patterns.

\section{Blockchain Relevance}
\label{sec:blockchain_relevance}

This section connects the foundations from the previous sections to the goal of this thesis, which is detecting coordinated malicious behaviour on Ethereum.

The abstractions introduced above are not only mathematically convenient, but they also match the structure of Ethereum transaction data. As shown in the section on Ethereum fundamentals and in Section~\ref{sec:graph}, Ethereum transactions are directed interactions between addresses, and every interaction is recorded together with persistent metadata such as a timestamp, a value, and the gas usage. The consequence for this thesis is that Ethereum is not just a static graph, but a transaction graph that keeps changing over time.

This temporal aspect is not a minor detail. Prior work on Ethereum anomaly detection argues that the time-related nature of transactions makes transaction graphs well suited for temporal network analysis \citep{pohlmann2019anomaly}. Studies of money trails report that activities such as phishing, gambling and money laundering show distinct cyclic transfer patterns that can only be identified when the order of transactions is preserved \citep{lal2021money}. Other work shows that suspicious behaviour often appears as rapid transaction bursts, sudden volume changes and short-window interaction patterns that a static graph cannot fully recover \citep{temporal_sampling_ethereum, ghoshcatalog2025}. Once the graph from Section~\ref{sec:graph} is extended with timestamps, it becomes possible to analyse not only \emph{who} interacts with whom, but also \emph{when}, \emph{how often} and \emph{in which order}.

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:temporal_networks}, a static aggregation can show that a group of addresses is connected, but it cannot tell a slow, unrelated sequence of transfers apart from a tightly coordinated one that runs within minutes. This matters because coordinated malicious activity is rarely a single transaction. It usually consists of several transfers spread across multiple addresses, for example to hide the origin of funds, redistribute them or imitate legitimate activity.

For this thesis, this has a direct methodological consequence. The graph-theoretic
and temporal foundations from the previous sections turn raw transaction
histories into higher-level behavioural patterns. Candidate temporal motifs
describe recurring, time-local interaction structures, and local-role
fingerprints make these structures measurable for each address. Earlier work
already retains temporal information in Ethereum transaction networks
\citep{wu2020temporal}. The step taken here is to evaluate an explicit local-role
representation of that information, as set out in
Section~\ref{sec:related_position}.

This leads to the next step. If Ethereum is treated as a temporal transaction graph, the question is no longer only how addresses are connected, but which forms of coordinated malicious behaviour produce characteristic temporal interaction patterns. The following section therefore looks at coordinated malicious behaviour on Ethereum and describes the main categories considered in this thesis.

\subsection{Types of Coordinated Malicious Behaviour on Ethereum}
\label{sec:scam_types}
The Ethereum blockchain hosts a broad range of fraudulent and malicious activities, made possible by the combination of pseudo-anonymity, programmable smart contracts, and the irreversibility of on-chain transactions. A taxonomy of cybercrime on the Ethereum blockchain derived from 5,644 verified malicious addresses identifies 19 distinct categories of fraud, with the total stolen funds across the observation period reaching \$1.65 billion on-chain alone, a figure that exceeds self-reported losses to the United States Federal Trade Commission by a factor of almost 16, underscoring the scale of underreporting \citep{hornuf2025cybercrime}. Two sets of categories are distinguished below. The empirical comparison in RQ3 and RQ4 uses the nine exact Real-CATS labels that pass the minimum-size rule: Blackmail Scam, Contract Exploit Scam, Fake Returns Scam, Hack Scam, Impersonation Scam, NFT Airdrop Scam, Phishing Scam, Pigbutchering Scam, and Rug Pull Scam \citep{shi2025realcats}. Ponzi schemes, pump-and-dump schemes, money laundering, and giveaway scams remain useful examples of temporal mechanisms, but they are not treated as inferential Real-CATS classes in this thesis. Real-CATS labels reported behaviour types rather than independently verified coordination events, so coordination is a proposed network interpretation, not an additional ground-truth label.

\paragraph{Ponzi Schemes.}
Ponzi schemes are the largest category of on-chain cybercrime by aggregate stolen value, accounting for approximately 60\% of all funds transferred to verified malicious addresses on Ethereum \citep{hornuf2025cybercrime}. Operators promise high returns to investors and use incoming deposits from new participants to pay earlier ones, creating the appearance of a functioning investment operation \citep{bartoletti2020smart}. On Ethereum, Ponzi logic is often encoded directly in smart contracts, which execute the redistribution of funds automatically and transparently \citep{chen2020survey}. Funds may therefore flow from many participant addresses into a central contract and later be redistributed outward, producing plausible fan-in and fan-out candidate patterns. Real-CATS has no exact Ponzi label, however, and the thesis does not infer Ponzi membership from another category such as Investment Scam or Fake Returns Scam.

\paragraph{Phishing.}
Phishing attacks involve deceiving users into approving malicious smart contract interactions or transferring funds to attacker-controlled addresses \citep{chen2020phishing}. In the Ethereum context, phishing has evolved beyond simple address impersonation to include signature-based token approval theft, where a victim signs a transaction that grants the attacker unlimited transfer rights over their tokens \citep{hornuf2025cybercrime}. The coordination aspect manifests in the use of drainer contracts: a central attacker-controlled contract collects funds from many victims in rapid succession, often within short time windows, producing a repeated fan-in temporal pattern anchored at the malicious contract address.

\paragraph{Pump-and-Dump Schemes.}
Pump-and-dump schemes involve a coordinated group of addresses accumulating a low-liquidity token, artificially inflating its price through simultaneous buy activity, and then selling their holdings once uninformed traders enter the market \citep{li2021cryptocurrency, hornuf2025cybercrime}. The coordination is explicit and time-critical: the buy phase and the sell phase each involve multiple addresses acting within bounded periods in order to maximise price impact and exit before the market corrects. Such activity could produce dense, time-local transaction patterns, but Real-CATS has no pump-and-dump label. Its temporal window is therefore not estimated in RQ4.

\paragraph{Rug Pulls and Exit Scams.}
A rug pull occurs when the developers of a token project attract liquidity, typically by depositing token reserves into a decentralised exchange, and then abruptly withdraw all deposited funds, rendering the token worthless \citep{hornuf2025cybercrime, xia2021rugpull}. Exit scams follow a similar pattern at the level of initial coin offerings: funds raised from investors are transferred out of the project contract to attacker-controlled addresses immediately or within a short period after fundraising closes. Both patterns produce a concentrated outflow from a contract address to a small set of beneficiary addresses within a narrow time window, a temporal interaction structure that differs structurally from ordinary liquidity provision.

Temporal cycle analysis of Ethereum transactions has found cyclic transfer patterns associated with laundering activity \citep{lal2021money}, making it a candidate for temporal-motif analysis. Real-CATS has no money-laundering label, so this thesis does not estimate a laundering-specific window or compare it empirically with pump-and-dump activity.

\paragraph{Impersonation Scam.}
Impersonation scams use the identity of a trusted person, service, or project to
induce a victim to transfer assets or approve an interaction. They overlap with
the social mechanism of phishing, but Real-CATS records Impersonation Scam as a
separate exact label. Possible on-chain patterns include repeated victim-to-scammer
transfers and consolidation through common beneficiary addresses. The experiment
does not assume that either pattern must be present.

\paragraph{Contract Exploit Scam and Hack Scam.}
A contract exploit abuses a defect in deployed code, whereas the broader Hack Scam
label covers compromises whose entry point need not be a smart-contract flaw
\citep{shi2025realcats}. Both can be followed by rapid sweeps, relay transfers, or
distribution across token and internal-transaction layers. The two labels are not
merged in this thesis, because doing so would erase the distinction supplied by the
dataset before RQ3 tests whether their fingerprints can be separated.

\paragraph{Blackmail Scam and Fake Returns Scam.}
Blackmail scams demand payment through coercion, while fake-return schemes solicit
funds by promising a payment or investment return that is not delivered. Either may
produce repeated inbound transfers, but neither label prescribes one transaction
topology. Their temporal fingerprints are therefore learned from the recorded
histories rather than defined from a hand-written temporal template.

\paragraph{NFT Airdrop Scam.}
NFT airdrop scams use unsolicited or fraudulent token distributions to attract a
victim to a malicious interaction. Ethereum Request for Comments (ERC) token
standards define how these assets are represented and transferred. The
transactions can be concentrated in the ERC-721 and ERC-1155 layers and can
appear as distribution stars followed by later
victim interactions. Keeping the Real-CATS layers separate allows RQ3 to retain
this layer-specific information.

\paragraph{Pigbutchering Scam.}
Pigbutchering scams combine social engineering with a longer confidence-building
phase before repeated transfers are solicited. This can yield a transaction history
that is temporally more extended than a single theft event, but the duration is not
fixed in advance. RQ4 therefore selects windows from the data and tests whether
using a separate window for each category improves classification over one shared
window.

\vspace{0.5em}
The descriptions above motivate why temporal structure may be informative. They do
not define the fingerprints or their time windows. Table~\ref{tab:scam_types}
therefore summarises only the nine exact labels used for inference and states the
network pattern that each label could plausibly expose. These are hypotheses for
interpretation, not rules used to construct labels or features. The broader Ponzi,
pump-and-dump, money-laundering, and giveaway examples remain part of the domain
context, but no result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} assigns them an empirical
$\delta$.

\chapter{Method} 
\label{ch:method}

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system_overview}

This chapter describes how the detection approach is put into practice. The
method is implemented as a single program that turns raw Ethereum transaction
records into malicious-address scores and category-level comparisons. The
program is not used interactively. It runs a fixed sequence of steps, always in
the same order, so that the same data and the same settings always lead to the
same result.

The program runs in two phases. A \emph{preparation} phase reads the raw data
once, decides which addresses take part in the study, and fixes how they are
divided into a development set and a test set. An \emph{execution} phase then carries
out the actual detection on top of these prepared data. Keeping the two apart
means that the data and the evaluation setup are settled before any model is
trained, so the reported results cannot be shaped, even by accident, by later
changes to the data or the settings. The execution phase is checkpointed as
well. After an expensive subgraph count has been committed successfully, an
interrupted run verifies and reuses it instead of starting that count again.

Overall the program moves from raw transactions to results in eight steps, shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:method_steps}. The first two belong to the preparation phase,
the remaining six to the execution phase.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Loading the data.} The program reads the transaction records and
        the address labels of the chosen dataset. For the main dataset the
        transaction table is very large, with several hundred million transfers,
        so it is read in small batches instead of being held in memory all at
        once. The datasets are described in Section~\ref{sec:data_acquisition}.

  \item \textbf{Preparing the data.} Not every labelled address can be used. The
        program measures how active each address is and keeps only those with
        at least three incident transactions, as required by the activity rule. It also sets aside a small
        number of extremely active hub addresses, so that a few exchange-sized
        accounts cannot dominate the analysis. The remaining addresses are split
        into a development set, used for tuning, and a separate test set, used
        only for the final measurement. For each address, the program stores just
        the transactions that involve it and writes these tables to
        disk, so that every later step works on the same data.

  \item \textbf{Building the temporal graph.} The prepared transactions are
        loaded into a temporal graph, in which every address is a node and every
        transfer is a directed edge that carries the time at which it happened.
        Repeated transfers between the same two addresses are kept separately,
        because their number and their order define the candidate patterns.
        This step is described in Section~\ref{sec:graph_construction}.

  \item \textbf{Counting the candidate subgraphs.} The program first checks the
        counter against synthetic validation templates. It then counts how
        often each address participates in each retained local role.
        Because an occurrence only counts when its transfers fall within the
        selected time span, the counting is repeated for a range of time windows,
        from five minutes up to 30 days. This step is described in
        Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}. Each completed count is stored as an
        immutable checkpoint before the next one begins.

  \item \textbf{Building the fingerprints.} The local motif-role counts of an address form
        its fingerprint, a short list of numbers that records how often the
        address occupied each retained role. From the same transactions the program
        also computes a set of simpler behavioural features, such as how much
        value the address sent and received, how many partners it had, and how
        its activity was spread over time. This produces three views of every
        address: the simpler features on their own, the motif fingerprint on its
        own, and the two together.

  \item \textbf{Running the detection.} For each of the three views, the program
        trains a classifier to tell malicious and benign addresses apart. It
        uses the development set to choose the time window that works best and
        then measures how well the classifier performs on the untouched test
        set. For RQ3 and RQ4 the same idea is used, except that the classifier
        sorts criminal addresses into their behaviour category rather than making
        a malicious-or-benign decision. The features and models are described in
        Section~\ref{sec:detection_methods}.

  \item \textbf{Analysing the results.} The program then answers the research
        questions. It compares the three views to see whether the motif
        fingerprint improves detection over the simpler features (RQ1) and how
        the time window affects its usefulness for detection (RQ2). It then checks
        whether different categories of malicious behaviour have distinct
        fingerprints (RQ3), and whether selecting a separate window for each
        category improves classification over one shared window (RQ4). Each comparison is
        supported by statistical tests, so that a difference is not mistaken for
        chance, and the whole run is repeated under several orderings of
        transactions that share the same timestamp, to confirm that the outcome
        is stable. The evaluation is described in
        Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}.

  \item \textbf{Visualising the results.} Finally, the program writes the tables
        and figures used in the Results chapter, including the comparison of the
        three views, the performance curve across the different time windows, and
        for RQ3 and RQ4, the category fingerprints and the comparison between
        category-specific and shared windows.
\end{enumerate}

  phase. The remaining six cover graph construction, fingerprint extraction,

\section{Data Acquisition}
\label{sec:data_acquisition}

Studying coordinated malicious behaviour on-chain needs two things at once:
transaction data, and reliable labels that say which addresses are malicious.
This combination is scarce. Transactions are public, but the intent behind an
address can only be established off-chain, through abuse reports, exchange
records, or investigations, and such labels are rare and unevenly spread across
the network \citep{shi2025realcats}. To work around this, the reported
evaluation uses a main Ethereum dataset and the category-labelled Real-CATS
dataset. Separate synthetic templates check the correctness of subgraph
counting. The pipeline also supports BLTE as an optional secondary
check, but no BLTE result is used to answer the research questions in this
thesis. The origin and prior use of the two published datasets, BLTE and
Real-CATS, were already covered in Section~\ref{sec:relwork_datasets}. Here each
real-world dataset is described by its source, its contents, and its
suitability for this thesis. Validation templates and the distinction between
predictive tests and network null models are described separately below.

\subsection{Real-World Datasets}

\paragraph{Hugging Face (HF) Ethereum Fraud Dataset.}
\label{sec:dataset_hf}
\textit{Source.} The dataset is published on the Hugging Face Hub
\citep{fesevu2025ethfraud}. This thesis uses its full-history release, which
pairs a complete transaction table with a set of labelled addresses, rather than
one of the smaller monthly extracts.

\textit{Contents.} The dataset has two parts. The first is a transaction table
of 380{,}566{,}661 transfers recorded between August 2015 and August 2025. Each
transfer carries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the sending and receiving address, as hashed identifiers,
  \item the timestamp of the transfer,
  \item the transferred value,
  \item the transaction hash and block number,
  \item a flag marking contract-creation transactions, and
  \item the transaction fee.
\end{itemize}
The second part is a label table of 114{,}739 addresses, of which 60{,}086 are
benign and 54{,}653 fraudulent. Each labelled address also states whether it is
a contract or an ordinary account, and carries a short text description of the
reported activity.

\textit{Suitability.} This is the main dataset of the thesis, and it carries the
evaluation for RQ1 and RQ2. It is the only dataset used here that
combines full timestamped transaction histories with a large and roughly
balanced set of malicious and benign labels. The timestamps matter because
temporal motifs depend on the exact timing and order of transfers, and the size
of the label set is large enough to draw more than one cohort of addresses while
still keeping an untouched test set aside.

\paragraph{BLTE.}
\label{sec:dataset_blte}
\textit{Source.} BLTE was introduced by \citet{alemari2021labeled} and is built
on the Ethereum Classic chain. Its origin and the results reported on it by
earlier work were described in Section~\ref{sec:relwork_datasets}. Here it is
used as obtained from the public release.

\textit{Contents.} The dataset contains 71{,}250 transactions from October 2017
to December 2019, labelled one transaction at a time as normal or abnormal.
There are 57{,}000 normal and 14{,}250 abnormal transactions, so about one
transaction in five is abnormal. Each record provides:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the transaction hash and nonce,
  \item the sending and receiving address,
  \item the transferred value, the gas, and the gas price,
  \item the block timestamp, block number, and block hash, and
  \item four labelling fields that mark whether the sender or the receiver is a
        known scam, and of which category.
\end{itemize}
A set of pre-computed per-address features is distributed alongside the raw
transactions, but this thesis does not use them, for the reason given below.

\textit{Suitability.} BLTE can be used as an optional secondary check of whether
the main finding also appears in another Ethereum-based dataset. It is less
directly comparable with the main dataset because its labels apply to
transactions rather than addresses and because only 169 of its 73{,}034 active
addresses are marked as scams. A BLTE run is therefore kept separate from the
main evaluation and uses class weighting to account for this imbalance. The
pre-computed features are left aside so that the same behavioural features can
be computed as for the other datasets. No completed BLTE result is included in
the Results chapter, and BLTE is not needed to answer any of the four research
questions.

\paragraph{Real-CATS.}
\label{sec:dataset_realcats}
\textit{Source.} Real-CATS was introduced by \citet{shi2025realcats}. Its design
and the sources of its labels were described in
Section~\ref{sec:relwork_datasets}. This thesis uses its Ethereum portion
together with the detailed transaction records released alongside it.

\textit{Contents.} The Ethereum portion covers 12{,}572 criminal and 16{,}019
benign addresses. For each address the dataset provides its transaction history,
split into five separate layers: ordinary transfers, internal transfers, and
ERC-20, ERC-721, and ERC-1155 token transfers. What sets Real-CATS apart from
the other datasets is that its criminal addresses carry a specific category,
rather than a single malicious flag. The categories are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Phishing Scam and Impersonation Scam,
  \item Contract Exploit Scam and Hack Scam,
  \item Blackmail Scam and Fake Returns Scam,
  \item Rug Pull Scam, NFT Airdrop Scam, and Pigbutchering Scam.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Suitability.} Real-CATS is the dataset used for RQ3 and RQ4. Its detailed
categories are what make both questions possible, because they allow the motif
fingerprints of different reported malicious behaviours to be compared directly,
rather than only a single malicious-versus-benign split. The labels do not state
whether an address belongs to a coordinated campaign, so the experiment tests
category-level fingerprint differences rather than independently verified
coordination. The labels are also
independent of the main dataset, since they come from public abuse reports
instead of the process behind the Hugging Face release. The categories are very
unevenly filled, with phishing and impersonation together accounting for most of
the criminal addresses, so RQ3 and RQ4 are limited to the categories that have
enough addresses to support a fair comparison and treat the smaller ones as
descriptive only. The catch-all category \emph{Other} is excluded as well, even
though it is large enough to qualify, because it collects unrelated reports and
therefore does not describe one kind of behaviour.

\subsection{Validation Templates and Null Models}
\label{sec:validation_and_null_models}

\paragraph{Synthetic validation templates.}
\label{sec:dataset_synthetic}
The validation data is generated in code. Each template contains exactly three
directed transfers on two or three nodes, with known directions and timestamps
and no additional background edges. The templates systematically cover the
patterns accepted by the counter. They do not simulate complete fraud campaigns
or provide malicious-address labels. A three-node structure may resemble one
part of a plausible scam, such as collecting and forwarding funds, but it
remains a candidate motif before any statistical comparison.

Raphtory returns an ordered vector of 40 local motif-role counts for each
address. A position means one component of that vector, not one of 40 global
patterns. The templates check all 24 star-centre roles, eight two-node roles,
and eight triangle roles. The difference from the 36 global patterns of
\citet{paranjape2017motifs} is explained in
Section~\ref{sec:motifcountingandretainedroles}. The templates also check
transfers exactly at a window boundary and transfers with equal timestamps.
Their purpose is to confirm that subgraph counting recognises a known two- or
three-node structure and assigns the expected local role counts. They are
never used to measure detection performance or compared with real-world
datasets as examples of fraud.

\paragraph{Network null models.}
A validation template has a known answer for the counter. A network null model
serves a different purpose: it generates random reference networks against which
the observed frequency of a candidate pattern can be tested
\citep{milo2002network}. For example, shuffling timestamps while retaining the
observed directed transfers would test their temporal arrangement conditional
on that fixed structure. The choice of preserved properties determines the
claim that such a comparison can support.

The completed experiments do not include a random-network null model, so no
individual candidate is claimed to be statistically overrepresented. The
permutation and bootstrap procedures in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol} instead assess predictive differences and
category separability. The alternative orderings of equal-timestamp transfers
are sensitivity checks, not random-network references.

\section{Temporal Graph Construction}
\label{sec:graph_construction}

The prepared transactions are turned into a temporal graph on which candidate
subgraphs are later counted. This section describes the graph model, how the
graph is actually built from each dataset, and the simple features that are read
straight from it and later serve as a comparison point for the fingerprints.

\subsection{Graph Model}

Each address is represented as a node and each transfer as a directed edge that
carries the time at which it happened. The result is a temporal directed
multigraph
\[
  \mathcal{G}=(V,E,\tau,w),
\]
where $V$ is the set of addresses, $E$ the set of transfers, $\tau(e)$ the
timestamp of transfer $e$, and $w(e)$ its transferred value. Two properties are
kept on purpose. Parallel transfers between the same pair of addresses are not
merged, because how many there are and in which order they occur is exactly what
the candidate patterns are built from. Edge direction is kept for the same reason, since an
address that only receives, one that only sends, and one that does both leave
different local role counts behind.

\subsection{Building the Graph from the Data}

Hugging Face and Real-CATS are stored in very different shapes, so the graph is
assembled differently for each, but always without holding more data in memory
than needed.

For Hugging Face the transaction table is far too large to load as a whole, with
more than 380 million transfers. It is therefore read in small batches. The
first pass measures how active each labelled address is and keeps every transfer
that touches a selected target. These incident transfers define the baseline and
the set containing each target and all of its direct partners. A second full
scan keeps every published transfer whose two endpoints both belong to that set.
The resulting released-neighbourhood closure is used for subgraph counting.

Every triangle involving a selected target consists of two target-to-partner
edges and one edge between those partners. The first two edges place both
partners in the retained node set, and the second scan then keeps their connecting
edge. Triangle counts are therefore complete relative to the published release.
They are not claimed to be complete for Ethereum activity absent from the
release. Baseline features remain restricted to target-incident transfers so
that adding the closure does not give the comparison model extra network scope.

Real-CATS is stored as one transaction history per address, and the same
transfer can appear in the histories of several addresses. These repeated copies
are removed once, using a disk-backed index so that the de-duplication does not
depend on everything fitting in memory. Each history is also split into five
layers, namely ordinary transfers, internal transfers, and ERC-20, ERC-721 and
ERC-1155 token transfers. Every layer becomes its own set of edges, and candidate subgraphs
are counted within each layer. This preserves the distinction between transaction
types, but excludes candidate sequences whose edges cross between layers.

\subsection{The Non-Motif Baseline}

RQ1 asks whether motif fingerprints add anything beyond ordinary address behaviour, so it
needs a description of each address that does not use motifs at all. This
baseline is computed from the same transfers and the same addresses as the motif
fingerprints. Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_features} lists the resulting 17
features, grouped into how active an address is, how much value it moves, the
structure of its immediate neighbourhood, and how its activity is spread over
time.

\section{Temporal Motif Extraction}
\label{sec:motif_extraction}

The baseline describes an address by totals, averages, and timing summaries.
It does not retain the full order of transfers between particular partners.
Two addresses can have the same number of
transfers, the same partners, and the same active span, yet behave very
differently: one might receive money from many senders within minutes and pass
it straight on, while the other collects the same money slowly over months.
Candidate temporal patterns capture these differences. Recall from
Section~\ref{sec:motifs} that a candidate is a short, time-ordered sequence of
transfers that all fall within a window of length $\delta$. Choosing a focal
address determines its local role in an occurrence. Stacking the address's
local role counts into a vector gives its motif fingerprint
(Equation~\ref{eq:motif_fingerprint}). This section describes how those
fingerprints are counted and turned into features.

\subsection{Subgraph Counting and Retained Local Roles}
\label{sec:motifcountingandretainedroles}

The counting itself is done by Raphtory, whose
\texttt{local\_temporal\_three\_node\_motifs} routine is written in Rust and
processes addresses in parallel. For every address it returns a \emph{local}
count for each motif role, that is, the number of candidate occurrences in
which the address plays that particular part. A star pattern is counted only for
the address at its centre, and a two-node pattern for both of the addresses
involved.

Raphtory reports 40 such roles per address: 24 star roles, 8 two-node roles, and
8 triangle roles. The star roles comprise three classes of eight in-and-out
direction patterns each. The four global two-node patterns each have two
endpoint roles, giving eight local two-node counts. This accounts for the
difference between 36 global patterns and 40 local role features. The output
is not a catalogue of 36 global motifs with four additional patterns.
The global patterns are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:motifs_paranjape}. The final Hugging Face experiment retains all
40 roles because its released-neighbourhood closure supports triangle counting.
It also evaluates the 32 star and two-node roles separately to measure whether
triangles add signal. Real-CATS retains those 32 roles per layer because its
collected histories cannot guarantee the edge between two partners. An address
that takes part in no retained candidate pattern keeps a fingerprint of all zeros rather
than being dropped from the study.

The retained roles fall into a few structural classes, each with a
straightforward reading in a transaction network.
Table~\ref{tab:motif_classes} lists them together with the kind of coordinated
behaviour they could represent. These interpretations do not label an occurrence
as fraudulent. In every example, the timing condition is
$t_3-t_1 \leq \delta$, with $\delta$ drawn from the five-minute to 30-day grid
in Section~\ref{sec:temporal_window_sweep}. There is no separate threshold for
\emph{rapid} activity, and an occurrence admitted at 30 days need not be rapid in
an everyday sense.

\subsection{Ordering Transfers with Equal Timestamps}

Because motifs are ordered sequences, the transfers have to be put in a definite
order before they can be counted. Time supplies most of that order, but many
transfers carry the same timestamp because they sit in the same block, and time
alone cannot separate them. When the data records where a transfer sits within its
block, that finer order is used. The Hugging Face data does not record this, so its
transfers that share a timestamp have no fixed order. To make sure the results do
not rest on an arbitrary choice, the whole analysis is run under seven versions of
the order. All orderings preserve the order of transfers with distinct
timestamps. They differ only in the order assigned to transfers sharing the
same timestamp: one fixed order, its reversal within each timestamp group, and
five random shuffles within those groups. These differences can change which
candidate temporal pattern a sequence matches and which local roles are counted.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol} reports whether the conclusions hold under
all seven. The order is computed without loading the whole table into memory, and
the sorted transfers are passed to Raphtory in large batches.

\subsection{Fingerprint Representations}

The retained counts are stored under stable names such as \texttt{star\_pre\_iii},
\texttt{two\_node\_ioi}, and \texttt{triangle\_1}. For RQ1 and RQ2 each count
$c$ is replaced by $\log(1+c)$. Some highly active addresses have much larger
counts than most targets.
Taking the logarithm compresses this range, and adding one first means that a
count of zero stays zero. The final Hugging Face detection step uses all 40
transformed values, either on their own or joined to the baseline of
Section~\ref{sec:graph_construction}. A parallel 32-role representation and a
triangles-only representation support the ablation analysis. How the fingerprint
and baseline are combined into the RQ1 feature sets is described in
Section~\ref{sec:detection_methods}.

The representation comparisons also use \emph{motif volume}, defined here as
the sum of an address's retained local role counts. This is a local summary,
not the number of distinct global pattern types.

RQ3 and RQ4 keep the five Real-CATS layers separate. Because the layers differ greatly
in how many transfers they contain, comparing raw counts would let the busiest
layer dominate. Instead, within each layer the counts are turned into proportions by dividing each
count by the layer total, and the square root of each proportion is taken. This is
known as a Hellinger transform \citep{legendre2001transformations}:
\[
    h_{v,j,l}^{\delta}
      = \sqrt{\frac{c_{v,j,l}^{\delta}}{\sum_{k=1}^{32}c_{v,k,l}^{\delta}}},
\]
where $c_{v,j,l}^{\delta}$ is the count of local role $j$ for address $v$ in
layer $l$ at window $\delta$. The expression applies when the layer's total
role count is positive. Using proportions removes the total count, so only
the mix of local roles remains, and
the square root keeps a few large proportions from dominating, much as the
logarithm does for the counts. A layer with no retained candidate occurrences is
kept as all zeros, including a layer that has transfers but no complete
three-edge pattern. The
same counts in log form, as used for RQ1 and RQ2, are also kept as a check.
Concatenating the five layers gives 160 local role features for each Real-CATS
address, with 32 components per layer.

For every window the pipeline additionally records whether each address has any
nonzero count at all. This coverage measure shows how the availability of
fingerprints changes with the window. Reporting coverage alongside performance
helps assess their relationship, but does not isolate whether a score increase
comes from lower sparsity or a more informative time scale.

\subsection{Temporal-Window Sweep}
\label{sec:temporal_window_sweep}

The counting is repeated over a fixed grid of nine windows,
\[
    \delta \in \{300,\,1800,\,3600,\,21600,\,86400,\,259200,\,604800,\,
                  1209600,\,2592000\}
    \text{ seconds},
\]
which correspond to five minutes, 30 minutes, one hour, six hours, one day, three
days, one week, two weeks, and 30 days. All windows use the same addresses and
the same train and test split, and the window is chosen only on the development
data, as described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}. RQ2 studies how
detection changes across this grid, and RQ4 asks whether separate windows for
each category improve classification over one shared window. A selected window
is the maximum span allowed for a three-edge candidate motif. It is not an estimate of the
total duration of a fraud campaign.

\section{Detection Methods}
\label{sec:detection_methods}

The previous two sections produced two descriptions of every address: the
non-motif baseline of Section~\ref{sec:graph_construction} and the motif
fingerprint of Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}. This section describes how
those descriptions are turned into a detector and which models are used. The
binary detection for RQ1 and RQ2 is kept apart from the category detection for
RQ3 and RQ4, because the two groups ask different questions and use different
datasets.

\subsection{The Three Feature Views}

RQ1 asks whether the motif fingerprint adds anything beyond ordinary address
behaviour. The cleanest way to answer this is a controlled comparison, in which
only the features change and everything else stays fixed. Three views of each
address are therefore built from the two descriptions above:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{baseline} view, the 17 non-motif features of
        Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_features},
  \item the \emph{motifs} view, the log-transformed local motif-role counts, with 32 roles
        in two Hugging Face samples and all 40 roles in the sample that includes
        triangles, and
  \item the \emph{combined} view, the two simply placed side by side.
\end{itemize}
As described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}, RQ1 and RQ2 use three
non-overlapping Hugging Face samples. Two samples use the 32 star and two-node
roles. The remaining sample has the released-neighbourhood closure needed for all
40 roles and evaluates the 32-role view and the eight triangle roles as separate
ablations.
All three views use exactly the same addresses and the same train and test split,
and no address is dropped just because its fingerprint is all zeros. Because the data is
held constant, this comparison evaluates the predictive value added by the
fingerprint representation. The main contrast is 17 baseline features against
those same 17 features plus 32 or 40 local motif-role features. Each role
combines structure, direction, focal-node position, and temporal order.
The experiment therefore does not isolate the contribution of temporal order
alone. That would require a matching representation with the ordering removed.

RQ2 then asks how the time window affects the usefulness of the fingerprints.
For this the
combined view is evaluated at each of the nine windows from
Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}. The baseline does not depend on the window, so
it stays the same throughout and serves as a fixed reference line.

\subsection{Classifiers for RQ1 and RQ2}

The detector for the binary questions is a random forest, an ensemble of decision
trees that each vote on whether an address is malicious. A random forest suits
this task because it can pick up interactions between different motif roles
without any manual feature scaling, and because it is a strong, well-understood
default rather than a model tuned hard for the highest possible score. Its
settings are fixed in advance, with 100 trees, a limited depth, balanced class
weights, and a fixed random seed, and they are not adjusted on the test data. The
forest is trained on the development addresses and then scores each address in the
test set with a probability of being malicious. The time window is chosen on the
development data by cross-validation (CV), using the rule described in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}.

As a robustness check, an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model is also fitted, on
the combined view at the chosen window. If it agrees with the random forest, that
strengthens confidence in the result, but the RQ1 decision always rests on the
random forest, which is fixed beforehand. The models use the allocated central
processing unit (CPU) threads. Table~\ref{tab:results_models}
summarises all three models.

\subsection{Classifier for RQ3 and RQ4}

RQ3 and RQ4 do not separate malicious from benign addresses. They sort criminal
addresses into their behaviour category, so the model has one class per category
rather than two. Both questions are answered from the same model on the same
data, and they are therefore always prepared and run together as one analysis.
Only categories with at least 100 addresses take part, because the categories are
very unevenly filled, as explained in Section~\ref{sec:data_acquisition}, and
categories are never merged into larger groups.

The input here is the per-layer fingerprint of
Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}, expressed as local role proportions, which captures
the mix of roles an address occupies in each transaction layer. The model is a
logistic regression in its multi-class form. Its inputs are first put on a common
scale, and this scaling is learned only from the training data so that the test
data cannot influence it. A logistic regression is chosen deliberately, because it
is a simple model with few parameters. This tests whether the motif proportions
alone can separate the categories, without a more complex model that might overfit
the smaller categories.

RQ3 evaluates category separability using motif fingerprints alone. Class
shares and a permutation reference provide context for this separation.
There is no comparison with the conventional behavioural features for category
classification, so RQ3 does not test whether fingerprints outperform those
features.

Two kinds of window are selected on the development data, one for each question. A
single global window is chosen to separate all categories as well as possible at
once, and this is the window RQ3 uses to ask whether the categories have distinct
fingerprints at all. In addition, a separate window is chosen for each category,
the one at which that category is recognised best. Whether these category-specific
windows actually beat the single global window is what RQ4 tests. Both kinds of
window are chosen on the development data only. The final model is refitted on all
development addresses and gives each address in the test set one probability per
category.

\subsection{Handling Class Imbalance}

The classes are uneven to different degrees across the experiments, only
slightly for the main dataset and strongly for the Real-CATS categories. The
same applies if the optional BLTE run is performed. This is handled in a single
way, by weighting each class in inverse proportion to how often it appears, so
that a rare class counts for as much as a common one during training. The random
forest and the logistic regression use balanced class weights, and the
gradient-boosted model uses the matching positive-class weight from its training
split. No artificial examples are added, and the number of addresses in each
class is left unchanged.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation_protocol}

\subsection{Scope}

The evaluation measures how well an address can be classified after the fact, from
its full recorded history. It does not estimate how early an address could have
been caught, and it makes no causal claim about how a fraud was carried out. As
described in Section~\ref{sec:system_overview}, the data and every setting are
fixed in a preparation step before any model is trained, so nothing in the
evaluation can be tuned on the test data. The parts that enforce this, such as the
recorded settings and the checks that the code and data have not changed, are
described in Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\subsection{Cohorts and Splits}

RQ1 and RQ2 are evaluated on three non-overlapping samples from the Hugging Face
dataset. Each sample contains two separate groups of addresses. An address is
eligible only if it takes part in at least three transactions. This is the
study's activity threshold; it is not a mathematical minimum for every local
role, since a node can participate in a triangle through two incident transfers.
Before the groups are drawn, the
most extreme addresses are removed: within each combination of label, contract
type, and activity level, the top 0.5\,\% most active addresses are dropped.
Without this, a few exchange-scale hubs would dominate the graph on their own. The
cut is made within each combination rather than across the whole dataset, because
the busiest addresses are almost all benign exchange wallets, so a single global
cut would remove far more benign than fraudulent addresses and quietly shift the
balance between the classes. The rule is fixed before any model is trained, and
every removed address is recorded.

The two groups serve different purposes. The \emph{prevalence} cohort is a plain
random sample of up to 5{,}000 eligible addresses, which keeps the natural mix of
fraudulent and benign addresses and checks that the findings hold on a realistic
distribution. The \emph{controlled} cohort is drawn from the remaining addresses,
up to 250 per combination of label, contract type, and activity level, which
balances the classes and holds activity and contract type roughly constant so that
a difference cannot be explained by those alone. The two cohorts within a
sample share no addresses and are reported separately. After both cohorts are
drawn, their addresses and known campaign groups are removed from the eligible
pool before the next sample is created. Repeating this procedure produces three
samples without reusing addresses or known campaigns.

Splitting the addresses needs one precaution. Some fraud arrives in campaigns of
many near-identical addresses, and if some of them landed in the training set
while their near-twins landed in the test set, the detector would look better than
it really is. Campaign groups are therefore kept together, and every split keeps
a whole group on a single side. In the implementation, these groups are defined
from the published address descriptions. After trimming whitespace and converting
the text to lower case, descriptions beginning with
\emph{multichain hack alert}, \emph{upbit hacker}, or
\emph{sorbet finance hack alert} are assigned to their respective shared
family. Every other address receives its own group. These are description-based
grouping rules, not a complete reconstruction of coordinated campaigns.
Each cohort is divided about
80 to 20 into a development set and a test set with a group-aware, stratified
split, and the development set is divided again into repeated five-fold splits,
under different random seeds, for choosing the time window. Cohort membership is
included in the stratification, while campaign assignments are made across both
cohorts together. A campaign represented in both cohorts therefore remains on
the same side and in the same validation fold. RQ3 and RQ4 share their own 80 to
20 split, stratified by category, again keeping only categories with at least 100
addresses and never merging them.

\subsection{Metrics and Window Selection}

The main measure is average precision (AP), which summarises how well the detector
ranks malicious addresses above benign ones across all decision thresholds. It
suits imbalanced data because it focuses on the rarer positive class. The F1
score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. These three measures are
reported as secondary measures at a fixed threshold of
0.5, which is never tuned on the test set. The area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) is reported alongside them.
For RQ3 and RQ4 the matching measures are the macro-averaged and per-category
average precision, the macro F1, and a confusion matrix.

The time window is chosen on the development data alone. Each window is scored by
cross-validation, and the window with the highest average score is taken. If
several windows share that score, the shortest of them is used, so that the choice
is decided in advance rather than by chance. As a check on how firm that choice is,
the shortest window whose score is still within one standard error of the best is
recorded alongside it as the one-standard-error (1-SE) choice, which shows whether a shorter window would have done
essentially as well. The test set plays no part in either.

\subsection{Statistical Tests}

For RQ1 the effect is the difference in test-set average precision between the
combined view and the baseline. Two procedures support it. The first is a
paired permutation test. For each campaign group, a random choice determines
whether the baseline and combined prediction scores are exchanged for every
address in that group. Address labels, campaign membership, and the train and
test assignments stay fixed. The models are not retrained. The difference in
AP is recomputed for 10{,}000 such exchanges, giving a one-sided $p$-value under
the null that the two sets of prediction scores are exchangeable within groups.

The second procedure is a bootstrap with 10{,}000 repetitions. Groups are
sampled with replacement separately within the benign and malicious classes.
Each class retains its original number of sampled groups. Every sampled group
contributes all of its addresses, including repeated copies if the group is
drawn more than once. The paired predictions are kept together and the AP
difference is recomputed, giving a 95\,\% confidence interval (CI).
The groups are the description-based families and singleton addresses defined
above.

Groups can contain different numbers of addresses. Each group has the same
chance of being sampled within its class, but a larger sampled group contributes
more addresses to AP. The total number of addresses and the class proportions
can therefore vary between bootstrap repetitions. The score-swap test, in
contrast, always uses the original test addresses. Both procedures preserve
dependence within the known groups, while the reported metric remains an
address-level measure. In the binary Real-CATS check, groups are individual
addresses because campaign identifiers are unavailable.

For RQ2 the chosen window is compared with each of the other windows using the
same kind of bootstrap. Because many windows are compared at once, the resulting
$p$-values are adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini--Hochberg
procedure (BH). The development curve and fingerprint coverage describe how
usefulness changes with $\delta$. The pairwise tests additionally check whether
the selected window is distinguishable from the alternatives. A unique best
window within the tested grid is supported only if it beats every alternative
after correction.

RQ3 asks whether the fingerprints contain category information. The held-out
category labels are shuffled 10{,}000 times while the fitted model and its
prediction probabilities stay fixed. Each shuffle preserves the class sizes.
The resulting macro AP values form a reference for no association between the
predictions and the category labels. Failure to reject this null would mean
insufficient evidence of category separation in this test. It would not prove
that the fingerprints contain no category information.

RQ4 asks whether the categories are better served by their own windows than by one
shared window. The two settings are compared on the test set in two ways. The first
is an overall comparison: the model using each category's own window is set against
the model using the single global window, and the difference in macro-averaged
average precision is measured with a paired bootstrap. The second looks at each
category on its own, comparing its own window with the global one, and because this
is repeated for every category the results are adjusted for multiple testing. The
number of distinct windows the categories select is reported alongside, since
category-specific windows can only help if the categories do not all choose the
same one.

\subsection{Robustness to Event Ordering}

Every result is produced under each of the seven orderings of
Section~\ref{sec:motif_extraction}. An ordering-dependent conclusion, such as an
RQ1 improvement or an RQ2 best window, is accepted only when its direction and its
decision agree across all seven orderings. This shows that a conclusion does not
depend on how transfers sharing a timestamp are ordered. It does not recover the
real order of those transfers, which the data does not record in the first place.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The study is built around a strict separation between fixing the experiment and
running it, so that no choice can be influenced by the test results. All settings live
in one place, \texttt{src/experiments/constants.py}, and are copied into the run's
protocol when preparation finishes. The main ones are shown below.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,
  caption={Selected fixed experiment settings.},
  label={lst:config}]
RANDOM_SEED = 42
CV_SEEDS = (42, 43, 44)
DELTAS = (300, 1800, 3600, 21600,
          86400, 259200, 604800,
          1209600, 2592000)
N_PERMUTATIONS = N_BOOTSTRAPS = 10_000
HF_COHORT_ELIGIBILITY = {
    "minimum_incident_edges": 3,
    "activity_upper_quantile": 0.995,
}
\end{lstlisting}

When preparation finishes it writes \texttt{protocol.json}, which records the data
(the source revisions and a checksum of the source, the prepared files, the exact
target addresses, and the cohort and fold assignments), every model, window,
ordering, resampling, and seed value, and checksums of the code and of the
installed package and Python versions. A checksum is a short code computed from a
file that changes whenever the file changes, so it flags any later edit. From that
point the protocol and the prepared data are fixed. When a run is executed it
recomputes all of these checksums and refuses to produce a single feature or metric
if the code, the data, the versions, or the settings differ from what was locked. Fixed seeds make
the cohort sampling, the folds, and the resampling repeatable, and the
deterministic synthetic templates of Section~\ref{sec:dataset_synthetic} check that
the subgraph counter assigns the roles it should, including right at the edge of a
time window. The thesis profile fixes all settings above. A separate development
profile may reduce only the numbers of permutation and bootstrap repetitions and
is always marked non-citable.

Before execution, a read-only preflight checks the recorded random-access memory
(RAM) and disk budget
without creating a lock, log record, temporary directory, or timestamp change in
the run. Execution then records its operational settings in the immutable
\texttt{execution\_\allowbreak definition.json}. This second definition separates operational
choices, including the thread allocation, direct or coordinated mode, coordinator
identity, and concrete worker thread settings, from the analytical model
parameters in the protocol. A resumed run must use the same allocation. The
thread limits are set before importing NumPy, scikit-learn, or the native
libraries, which prevents numerical thread pools from silently exceeding that
allocation.

Exact equality is required for integer motif counts, address order, splits,
selected windows, and hypothesis decisions. Independently recomputed floating
probabilities and metrics are compared within a numerical tolerance because
Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS), XGBoost, and Parquet encoders need not be byte-identical under different
thread allocations. Reusing an existing verified checkpoint or stage, in
contrast, is byte-preserving. Portable Document Format (PDF) timestamps are
removed so their hashes do not
change only because of generation time.

Scientific guards run before finalisation. They reject overlapping or duplicate
targets, campaign groups that cross a split, inconsistent folds, missing
classes, negative motif counts, non-finite features or metrics, duplicate
prediction rows, probabilities outside $[0,1]$, and multi-class probability
vectors that do not sum to one within numerical tolerance. Conflicting
duplicate labels in Real-CATS are rejected, while identical duplicates are
collapsed and counted in the preparation audit. All five layer files are
required even when one is empty. Checkpoint content is hashed from a fixed
Arrow representation with named columns, 64-bit integer counts, and addresses
sorted by their 8-bit Unicode Transformation Format (UTF-8) bytes,
independently of its Parquet storage encoding.

\subsection{Result Artifacts}

Each run has its own directory that is never overwritten, so results from
different runs cannot be mixed, and a run may only create a file that does not yet
exist. Prepared edges, fingerprints, and test-set predictions are stored as
Parquet, the protocol and the test summaries as JavaScript Object Notation
(JSON), the chapter tables as comma-separated values (CSV),
and the figures as PDF. Output files are first fully written and synchronised in
a private attempt directory. Files are committed without replacement, and a
multi-file stage is published by an atomic directory rename after its manifest is
written last. The public \texttt{features}, \texttt{metrics}, and related
directories contain hard links to verified stage payloads, preserving the
filenames consumed by the Results chapter.

Preparation and execution have separate resume rules. During Real-CATS
preparation, the SQLite index records each completed address and can be resumed
only before the experiment settings are fixed. During execution, every attempt
has its own identifier and scratch directory. HF commits one subgraph-count
checkpoint for every ordering and window. Real-CATS commits one for every layer, ordering, and
window. A new attempt may reuse earlier valid commits. A completed run rejects
both a fresh execution and a resume.

The integrity chain is acyclic. The locked protocol and prepared data identify
the execution definition. These identify every checkpoint and stage. The
result inventory hashes the committed stage manifests and public files, and the
execution manifest records the inventory hash. Each inventory and manifest has
a separate checksum file and does not include itself. The final
\texttt{verify} command checks this chain and the scientific schemas without
writing to the run. Ordinary verification distinguishes a valid non-citable
development or smoke run from corruption. Final thesis acceptance additionally
requires \texttt{--require-citable}.

The only appended file is \texttt{run\_log.jsonl}, which stores one JSON record
per line in JSON Lines (JSONL) format. Its writes are thread-safe
and synchronised to disk. A native operation that runs for several minutes

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This thesis examined whether temporal motifs provide useful information for
detecting malicious Ethereum addresses and for distinguishing between different
categories of malicious behaviour. The results lead to one overall conclusion.
Motif fingerprints contain information about address behaviour,
but they do not reliably improve classification once conventional transaction
and graph features are already available. The temporal window has a clear effect
on how many motifs can be observed, but the experiments do not identify one
unique window that is best for every sample. The Real-CATS categories also have
detectable differences in their fingerprints, although these differences are
too weak for accurate category classification. Selecting a separate window for
each category does not improve this result.

These findings are discussed below in the same order as the research questions.
The chapter then considers their theoretical and practical implications and the
limitations of the study.

\section{Interpretation of the Main Findings}

\subsection{Detection Value of Temporal Motifs}

RQ1 concerns the complete fingerprint representation. The comparison tests
whether adding local motif-role features to the 17 conventional features improves
detection. Since the roles combine structure, direction, focal-node position,
and edge order, the result does not isolate a causal contribution of temporal
ordering.

The first result is that temporal motifs contain a useful signal, but that their
additional value depends on which other features are already present. Adding the
motif fingerprint to the baseline increases AP in all six Hugging Face
comparisons. Three of these increases are significant. The non-significant
comparisons include the prevalence cohort of one 32-role sample and both
cohorts of the 40-role analysis. In that analysis, the improvements are 0.0237 and
0.0161, but both confidence intervals include zero. The
binary Real-CATS comparison is even closer, with an AP difference of only
0.0011. It also produces a lower F1 score after the local motif-role features are added.
The positive direction is therefore not enough to conclude that motifs provide
a reliable improvement over the baseline.

This does not mean that the motif fingerprint contains no information. The
motif-only models can still rank addresses to some degree, and the Real-CATS
motif-only model reaches an AP of 0.8708. More importantly, the
role-by-role fingerprint performs better than total motif volume alone. In the
40-role Hugging Face analysis, this difference is 0.1173 AP in the controlled
cohort and 0.1528 AP in the prevalence cohort. This shows that the distribution
of activity across motif roles is more informative than the number of motifs by
itself.

The result changes once conventional features are added. A baseline supplemented
with motif composition and motif volume performs similarly to the full combined
model across all three Hugging Face samples. The detailed role counts therefore
contain a signal,
but much of that signal overlaps with information that can already be obtained
from simpler summaries. This overlap is plausible because many transaction
patterns affect several feature groups at the same time. A rapid fan-out, for
example, changes the motif fingerprint, but it can also increase the number of
outgoing transfers, the out-degree, and the number of transaction partners. The
comparison does not isolate the exact source of this overlap, but it shows that
the complete fingerprint adds little once these related properties are present.

The triangle analysis leads to the same interpretation. Extending the
Hugging Face graph makes it possible to retain all eight triangle roles, but
adding them changes AP by at most 0.0071 in the reported comparisons. None of
these differences is significant. Triangle motifs may still be useful in other
graphs or for other kinds of malicious behaviour. The present results only show
that they do not strengthen address detection in these cohorts.

The strong baseline performance is consistent with earlier Ethereum detection
work based on transaction aggregates and static graph features
\citep{chen2022phishing, sanjalawe2023atd}. The motif-only results also agree
with the observation of \citet{arnold2024motifs} that local motif signatures can
expose behavioural differences that disappear in global counts. This thesis
tests the next step by asking whether that information improves a detector on
held-out addresses. The comparison shows that a descriptive signal does not
automatically provide additional predictive value. The result
also differs from the improvement reported by \citet{liu2023temporal} on
non-blockchain financial networks. This suggests that results from one type of
transaction network do not necessarily transfer to another, especially when
the labels and baseline features are different.

Temporal motif features have also been used for Ethereum phishing detection
\citep{wang2023temporal}. The present results qualify the usefulness of this
representation under the labels and evaluation protocol used here. They do not
provide a direct comparison with that earlier detector.

\subsection{Choice of the Temporal Window}

RQ2 asks how window size affects fingerprint usefulness. Over the tested range,
longer windows generally increase both detection AP and the number of addresses
with a nonzero fingerprint. This answers the question without requiring a unique
optimum. The
40-role Hugging Face analysis selects 30 days for both cohorts, while two weeks
remains within one standard error of the maximum. Both 32-role controlled
cohorts select two weeks, and all three prevalence cohorts select 30 days. The
Real-CATS binary
comparison also selects 30 days, but a window of only three days is within one
standard error of its maximum. These results point towards a broader range of
longer windows rather than one clearly best setting.

Motif coverage helps explain this pattern. In the 40-role Hugging Face analysis,
coverage increases from 12.2\% to 77.7\% in the controlled cohort and from
11.1\% to 75.9\% in the prevalence cohort between five minutes and 30 days.
A short window leaves most
addresses with an all-zero fingerprint because their relevant transfers do not
occur close enough together. Increasing $\delta$ allows more three-edge
sequences to be counted and gives the model information for more addresses. The
parallel increase in coverage and AP suggests that part of the benefit of a
longer window comes from reduced sparsity. The experiment does not show how much
of the improvement is caused by coverage and how much comes from a more suitable
behavioural time scale.

This distinction is important for interpreting RQ2. A selected window defines
the maximum time span of one three-edge motif. It does not measure the duration
of a fraud campaign. A 30-day window can contain a rapid transaction sequence in
the same way as a five-minute window, but it also admits sequences whose edges
are much farther apart. The selected value therefore reflects a compromise
between observing enough motifs and keeping the events temporally close. Since
30 days is the upper boundary of the tested grid, the results also cannot show
whether performance would continue to increase at still larger windows. The
supported conclusion is limited to the tested range.

\subsection{Differences Between Behaviour Categories}

The category analysis uses motif fingerprints alone. Its class-share and
permutation references establish whether the fingerprints contain category
information. They do not establish whether this representation is more useful
than conventional behavioural features for category classification, which would
require an additional baseline.

RQ3 shows that the Real-CATS categories are not identical in their motif
fingerprints. At the shared two-week window, the model reaches a macro AP of
0.1953 compared with an average class share of 0.1111. The permutation test
finds the category signal under every timestamp ordering. This provides evidence
that the motif distributions contain information about the recorded behaviour
category.

The practical separation is much weaker than the permutation result alone might
suggest. Macro F1 is 0.1914, and the confusion matrix shows that many addresses
are assigned to the wrong category. The one-vs-rest AP values also need to be
read together with their class shares. Phishing Scam has the highest AP, but it
also represents more than two thirds of the sample. Several smaller categories
have only 25 to 47 addresses in the test set, which makes their individual
results less precise.

One likely reason for the limited separation is that a category label does not
define one transaction pattern. Two phishing addresses can use different
collection and withdrawal strategies, while an impersonation scam and a fake
returns scam can produce similar transfers despite having different social
engineering methods. The label describes the purpose of the malicious activity,
whereas the motif fingerprint describes local transaction structure. These two
descriptions are related, but they are not equivalent. The slightly higher AP of
the count-based model suggests that motif volume may also contain category
information. However, the comparison changes more than volume alone and does not
identify which part of the count representation produces the difference.

RQ4 further limits the interpretation of the category results. The development
data selects six different category-specific windows, ranging from six hours to
30 days. On the held-out test set, using these windows instead of the shared
two-week window changes macro AP by only 0.0009. The confidence interval includes
zero and none of the category-level differences is significant after correction
for multiple comparisons. Most selected windows also change when transactions
with the same timestamp are ordered differently.

The development maxima should therefore not be treated as stable properties of
the categories. Selecting the highest point from nine windows will produce a
maximum for every category even when several windows perform almost equally.
The held-out comparison tests whether these apparent differences generalise, and
in this study they do not. The category-specific windows also do not estimate how
long the corresponding scam types last. They only describe where the classifier
performed best on one development sample.

\section{Theoretical Implications}

One implication is that information content and incremental predictive value
are not the same. Temporal motifs can describe address behaviour
in a meaningful way even when they do not improve a classifier that already uses
strong conventional features. The motif-only and representation results show
that the role distribution contains information. The combined-model results show
that this information is partly redundant with transaction aggregates and static
graph properties. A representation can therefore be useful for describing a
network without being the best way to improve detection performance.

The results also clarify what can be attributed to temporal ordering. Each motif
role combines directed graph structure, the local position of the target address,
and the order of the three edges. The advantage over motif volume shows that the
detailed role distribution matters. It does not isolate temporal order from the
other parts of the representation. A direct comparison with otherwise identical
unordered motif features would be needed to measure the contribution of order on
its own. The present comparison instead evaluates the complete temporal-motif
representation against the non-motif baseline.

The results also clarify the meaning of $\delta$. The window is often
described as the time scale of a motif, but the results show that it also controls
whether a fingerprint can be observed at all. At short windows, sparsity is the
dominant problem for many addresses. At long windows, more sequences become
available, but transactions that are less closely related can also be combined.
This makes the window both a behavioural and a measurement parameter. The lack
of a unique optimum suggests that malicious behaviour may not operate at one
shared time scale. It also suggests that a representation combining several
windows could be more suitable than forcing every address into one value of
$\delta$. This possibility is not tested in the present analyses.

The category results raise another issue. Broad fraud labels do not map directly
to single network structures. Their fingerprints are distinguishable in
aggregate, but the overlap between categories remains large. Temporal motifs
therefore capture one part of malicious behaviour rather than a complete model
of it. Off-chain intent, the role of smart contracts, token type, and the wider
campaign context may distinguish two categories even when their local transfer
patterns look similar. Conversely, two addresses with the same category can
have different roles within a campaign and therefore different fingerprints.

These points narrow the claim made in this thesis. Temporal motifs do
retain structure that aggregate features cannot express directly. However, the
existence of this additional representation does not guarantee a large
classification gain. Its value depends on the baseline, the available graph,
the address activity, and the way malicious behaviour is labelled.

\section{Practical Implications}

For binary malicious-address detection on the datasets used here, the conventional
feature set remains the most practical starting point. It performs better than
the motif-only model and requires less computation than a sweep over temporal
windows and timestamp orderings. Adding motifs may provide a small improvement
in some samples, but the effect is not stable enough to justify treating the
combined model as generally superior.

The same cost consideration applies to triangles. Constructing the released
neighbourhood closure substantially increases the number of transfers that must
be processed. The resulting triangle roles do not provide a significant gain in
the final Hugging Face experiment. For a similar address-level detection task, it
may therefore be reasonable to begin with the 32 star and two-node roles and add
triangles only when the graph supports them and their contribution can be tested.
This is a conclusion about the current datasets rather than a recommendation to
exclude triangles from every blockchain analysis.

If motifs are used, the temporal window should be selected on development data
rather than chosen from an assumed scam duration. The one-standard-error results
also provide a practical alternative to selecting the numerical maximum. A
shorter window that performs nearly as well can reduce the amount of history
needed for an address and may reduce the counting workload. The current study
does not measure online latency, so it cannot determine the best operational
trade-off.

The fingerprints may still be useful as descriptive summaries for an analyst.
They show whether an address mainly participates in repeated two-node exchanges,
stars, or triangles, and whether its role is mainly incoming or outgoing. Such a
summary could help compare addresses or inspect a suspected campaign. This
possible use is not evaluated here. The evaluation measures classification
performance, not whether motif explanations help an investigator make better
decisions.

The Real-CATS results should also not be used as a stand-alone category detector.
The significant permutation result shows that category information exists, but
the low macro F1 shows that the predictions are not accurate enough for that use.
A practical system would need additional information beyond the motif
fingerprint, especially for the smaller categories.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Candidate patterns and network significance.}
The experiments count candidate temporal subgraphs and evaluate their local
role fingerprints as predictive features. No random-network reference is
generated, so the study cannot establish that any individual pattern is a
statistically overrepresented network motif. Synthetic templates validate the
counter, and prediction permutations test classification results. Neither
replaces a network null model.

\paragraph{Labels and unit of analysis.}
The study treats the published address labels as ground truth. These labels are
based on off-chain reports and investigations, which can be incomplete or
incorrect. An address label also describes the address rather than a complete
campaign. One campaign can use many addresses, and one address can perform only
one part of the wider behaviour. The Hugging Face splits keep known campaign
groups together where that information is available. Real-CATS does not provide
a campaign identifier, so its train and test sets can be separated by address
but not by campaign. Similar addresses from the same unknown campaign could
therefore appear in both sets.

\paragraph{Category size and heterogeneity.}
The Real-CATS category distribution is strongly imbalanced. Phishing Scam
dominates the data, while several categories contain only a small number of test
addresses. Macro metrics give every category equal weight, but they do not remove
the uncertainty caused by small samples. The exact labels are also broad and can
contain several transaction strategies. RQ3 therefore tests whether the recorded
categories are distinguishable on average. It does not show that every address
within one category shares a common motif fingerprint.

\paragraph{Graph coverage.}
The Hugging Face triangle counts are complete relative to the published release
and the released-neighbourhood closure, but not necessarily relative to the full
Ethereum chain. Transactions absent from the release cannot be recovered by the
pipeline. Real-CATS provides detailed per-address histories across five layers,
but these histories do not guarantee the partner-to-partner edge required for a
complete triangle. Its category analysis therefore uses only star and two-node
roles. The two datasets consequently do not provide exactly the same view of the
transaction graph.

\paragraph{Cohort composition.}
The three Hugging Face samples use non-overlapping addresses and known campaign
groups, which prevents their direct reuse between samples. Repeated exclusions
also change the population that remains. The 40-role controlled cohort
contains 503 malicious addresses and none of them is a contract account. Its
result cannot establish whether local motif-role features help detect malicious contracts.
Two samples use 32 motif roles, while the remaining sample uses all 40. The
analyses therefore test the same main question on different samples, but
they are not identical replications of one feature definition.

\paragraph{Time representation.}
Ethereum timestamps do not provide a unique order for every pair of transactions.
Several transfers can share the same timestamp, especially when they occur in
the same block. The seven ordering variants show that the main RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3
answers do not depend on one arbitrary tie order. They also show that most RQ4
window selections are not stable. The variants cannot recover the true order of
events when the source data does not record it.

The window grid is another limitation. It covers values from five minutes to 30
days, and 30 days is selected in the 40-role Hugging Face and binary Real-CATS
analyses. Since this value
lies at the boundary, the study cannot determine whether a still larger window
would perform better. The fixed grid also does not test every possible duration
between its nine values.

\paragraph{Retrospective evaluation.}
The task classifies an address after observing the transaction history available
in the dataset. It does not measure how early a malicious address could be
detected or how performance changes as the history grows. A longer window may be
useful in this retrospective setting while being too slow for an operational
warning system. The results should therefore not be interpreted as real-time
detection performance.

\paragraph{Models and external validation.}
The primary binary model is a random forest with fixed settings. XGBoost is used
as a robustness check, and the category task uses logistic regression. These
choices keep the comparison focused on the features, but they do not cover every
possible classifier or interaction between model and representation. A model
designed specifically for temporal graphs may use the motif information
differently.

The binary Real-CATS sample provides an external comparison, but its labels and
data construction differ from Hugging Face. BLTE remains optional and no completed
BLTE result is included. The evidence for generalisation is therefore limited to
one additional dataset. Results on other chains, time periods, and label sources
may differ.

Taken together, these limitations do not remove the observed motif signal, but
they restrict what can be concluded from it. The study supports temporal motifs
as a meaningful description of address behaviour. It does not show a reliable
improvement over conventional detection features, a universal temporal window,
or a practical stand-alone classifier for behaviour categories.


\chapter{Conclusion \& Outlook}
\label{ch:conclusion}
\section{Conclusion}

Malicious activity on Ethereum is difficult to detect from individual
transactions. An address can look ordinary on its own even when it is part of a
larger sequence involving several addresses. This thesis examined whether
temporal motifs make such patterns useful for detection. Each address was
represented by the roles it occupies in ordered three-edge patterns that occur
within a temporal window $\delta$. This representation was then compared with
conventional transaction aggregates and static graph features.

The main contribution is an evaluation of temporal motif fingerprints
on labelled Ethereum addresses. The evaluation covers binary detection on the
Hugging Face Ethereum Fraud Dataset and a separate Real-CATS sample, as well as
category classification across nine Real-CATS behaviour labels. The pipeline
counts the retained motif roles for several temporal windows, keeps the five
Real-CATS transaction layers separate, and accounts for transfers that share a
timestamp. All main comparisons use held-out addresses. This makes it possible
to test whether a visible motif signal also improves prediction on addresses
that were not used to select the model or temporal window.
The available labels identify malicious addresses and reported behaviour
categories rather than independently verified coordinated campaigns. The
evaluation therefore measures address-level behavioural signal, not direct
campaign detection.

The answer to RQ1 is that temporal motifs contain information, but do not
reliably improve malicious-address detection beyond the conventional baseline.
Adding the fingerprint increases average precision in all six Hugging Face
cohort comparisons, although only three of these increases are statistically
significant. In the 40-role Hugging Face analysis, the increases are 0.0237 in
the controlled cohort and 0.0161 in the prevalence cohort, with both confidence
intervals including zero. The binary Real-CATS comparison produces an increase
of only 0.0011 and a lower F1 score. At the same time, the role-by-role
fingerprint performs better than motif volume alone. Its advantage in the
40-role analysis is 0.1173 average precision in the controlled cohort and
0.1528 in the prevalence cohort. The way an address participates in motifs is
therefore more informative than the total number of motifs, but much of this
information overlaps with simpler address features.

For RQ2, increasing the temporal window generally gives higher average precision
and provides a non-zero
fingerprint for more addresses. In the 40-role analysis, motif
coverage rises from 12.2\% to 77.7\% in the controlled cohort and from
11.1\% to 75.9\% in the prevalence cohort between five minutes and 30 days.
The 30-day window gives
the highest development score in both cohorts, but two weeks remains within one
standard error of the maximum. For no Hugging Face sample is the selected maximum
significantly better than every other tested window under all timestamp
orderings. Since 30
days is also the largest tested value, it cannot be treated as a universal
optimum, and no unique optimum within the tested grid is established either.
The selected window describes the maximum span of a counted candidate pattern,
not the duration of a malicious
campaign.

RQ3 shows that the nine Real-CATS behaviour categories have detectable
differences in their motif fingerprints. At the shared two-week window, the
classifier reaches a macro average precision of 0.1953, compared with an
average class share of 0.1111. The permutation result remains significant under
all seven timestamp orderings. However, the macro F1 score is only 0.1914 and
the categories overlap strongly in the confusion matrix. The fingerprints
therefore contain category information, but they are not sufficient for
accurate category classification on their own. RQ3 establishes separability
within this representation, not superiority over conventional behavioural
features for category classification.

The answer to RQ4 is no. Replacing the shared two-week window with separately
selected category windows changes held-out macro average precision by only
0.0009. The confidence interval includes zero, and none of the category-level
changes is significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Most of the
selected windows also change when equal-timestamp transfers are ordered
differently. These development selections should not be interpreted as stable
time scales of the corresponding behaviour categories.

The central result of the thesis is therefore a distinction between behavioural
information and additional predictive value. Temporal motif fingerprints reveal
how an address participates in local, ordered transaction patterns. This signal
is visible in motif-only detection and in the comparison between Real-CATS
categories. It does not consistently improve a detector that already has access
to strong transaction aggregates and static graph features. Temporal motifs are
therefore useful as a description of address behaviour, but the results do not
support using them as a replacement for conventional features or as a generally
reliable improvement to them.

\section{Outlook}

The existing analysis keeps the five Real-CATS transaction layers separate, but
it counts motifs only within each layer. It therefore cannot represent one
ordered pattern whose edges cross from an ordinary transfer to an internal
transfer and then to a token transfer. The Hugging Face graph is limited to
ordinary transaction edges as well. This matters for behaviour that is carried
out through smart contracts, including contract exploits, token-based scams,
and contract-mediated Ponzi schemes. A useful next step is a typed, multi-layer
temporal graph in which the transaction type remains part of each motif. This
would show whether cross-layer sequences provide information beyond the
separate layer fingerprints already used in this thesis.

The temporal window also deserves a different treatment. The results suggest a
broad range of useful longer windows rather than one stable optimum. A
multi-scale fingerprint could combine short, medium, and long windows so that a
rapid sweep and a slower consolidation pattern are not forced into the same
value of $\delta$. The tested grid could also be extended beyond 30 days, since
its upper boundary is selected in several analyses. Any larger or multi-scale
representation would need to be selected on development data and compared with
the single-window fingerprint on held-out addresses. Otherwise, the additional
windows could increase model complexity without improving generalisation.

A further experiment should separate temporal order from graph structure more
directly. The current role catalogue combines direction, local node position,
topology, and event order. It can show that the complete fingerprint is useful,
but not how much of that usefulness comes from the order alone. Comparing it
with an otherwise identical unordered representation would help isolate the
contribution of temporal ordering. A separate random-network comparison could
shuffle timestamps while retaining the directed transfers, then compare the
candidate counts with their reference distributions. That experiment would test
overrepresentation conditional on the preserved graph structure, with correction
for the number of tested patterns and windows.

The current evaluation is retrospective. Every address is classified from the
history available in the dataset, so the results do not show how quickly a new
malicious address could be identified. A prospective evaluation could reveal
transactions gradually and produce a prediction after fixed time intervals or
after each new transfer. Detection performance could then be reported together
with the time and number of transactions needed before an address is detected.
This would provide a more useful test for an operational warning system than
classification from a complete history.

External validation should also be extended. Real-CATS provides an independent
binary comparison, but its labels and graph construction differ from the main
Hugging Face data. BLTE remains an optional check and its transaction-level
labels do not provide a direct equivalent to the address-level task. Future
work would benefit from additional address-labelled datasets from other time
periods and chains. Campaign identifiers would be especially valuable because
they would allow related addresses to remain together during evaluation and
would make it possible to study coordinated behaviour at campaign level rather
than inferring it from individual address labels.

Finally, the model comparison can be extended. A temporal graph model may be
able to use the underlying event sequence more directly than a random forest or
logistic regression. The fingerprint may also be useful as a compact
explanation for an analyst, even when it adds little to average precision. Both
uses require separate evaluation. A more complex model should be compared with
the same conventional baseline, and an explanatory use should be tested with
analysts rather than inferred from classification scores.

These extensions follow directly from the main finding. Temporal motifs do
capture meaningful transaction structure, but that structure only becomes
valuable for detection when it adds something that simpler features do not
already provide. Future work should therefore focus not only on counting more
motifs, but on identifying which temporal and cross-layer patterns contribute
new information on unseen addresses.
